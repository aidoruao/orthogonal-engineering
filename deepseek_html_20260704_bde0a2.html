🧊 The Simple Truth

HVAC is just moving heat — everything else is detail
Heat always moves from hot to cold.
That's the only rule you need to remember. Everything in HVAC is just creating a path for heat to follow — or blocking a path it wants to take.
The Cycle (4 steps)
1. Cold liquid absorbs heat indoors → boils to cold gas.
2. Cold gas gets compressed → becomes hot gas.
3. Hot gas dumps heat outdoors → condenses to warm liquid.
4. Warm liquid expands → becomes cold liquid again.
What veterans know
• 90% of problems are building problems, not equipment problems.
• If you stop the heat from getting in, the AC doesn't have to work as hard.
• Always check the building first — then the unit.

🔄 The Path — Cylinder → Indoor Coil → Compressor → Outdoor Coil → Back

What actually happens, step by step
1. Cylinder (or System Charge)
Refrigerant starts as a high‑pressure liquid in the cylinder or is already in the system. It enters the indoor coil through the expansion valve.
2. Indoor Coil (Evaporator)
The liquid is now at low pressure — so it's very cold. A fan blows warm house air over the coil. Heat moves from the air into the cold liquid. The liquid boils into a cold gas.
3. Compressor
The cold gas travels to the compressor. The compressor squeezes the gas — this makes it hot (hotter than the outside air).
4. Outdoor Coil (Condenser)
The hot gas flows through the outdoor coil. A fan blows outside air over it. Heat moves from the hot gas into the outside air. The gas condenses back into a warm liquid.
5. Back Indoors
The warm liquid travels back inside, passes through the expansion valve, drops in pressure, and becomes cold liquid again.
Repeat
That's it. That's the entire cycle. The refrigerant never leaves the sealed system — it just changes state (liquid ↔ gas) and temperature.
Key insight: The refrigerant is cold the entire time it's indoors. It only gets hot after the compressor squeezes it. That's the whole trick.

📜 History — What Changed and When

The events that burned veterans and shaped the industry
Pre‑1930s: Refrigerants were toxic or flammable — ammonia, sulfur dioxide, methyl chloride. Techs died regularly.
1930s: Freon (CFCs) invented by Thomas Midgley Jr. — safe, stable, non‑flammable. A revolution. No one knew it would destroy the ozone layer.
1970s‑1980s: Scientists discover CFCs are destroying the ozone layer. The Montreal Protocol (1987) phases them out.
1990s‑2000s: HCFCs (R‑22) replace CFCs — lower ODP, but still deplete ozone. HFCs (R‑410A, R‑134a) replace HCFCs — zero ODP, but high GWP.
2010s: Climate change becomes a focus. HFCs are found to be potent greenhouse gases. The Kigali Amendment (2016) phases down HFCs.
2020‑2026: A2L refrigerants (R‑32, R‑454B, R‑1234yf) become the new standard — very low GWP, mildly flammable. R‑22 production banned (2020). R‑410A new systems banned (2025/2026).
What burned veterans: Every time the refrigerant changed, the tools, oils, and procedures changed. Mineral oil → POE oil. Low‑pressure gauges → high‑pressure gauges. Non‑flammable → A2L safety protocols. The veterans who didn't adapt got left behind — or got hurt.

🤫 The Tacit Knowledge — What Veterans Assume You Know

The things no one tells you in school
Start with the building, not the unit.
If a room is hot, check windows, doors, insulation, and duct leaks before you even look at the compressor. 90% of problems are building problems.
Airflow is everything.
A perfectly charged system with bad airflow won't cool. Always check filters, ducts, and blower speed first.
Superheat and subcooling are your compass.
They tell you if the system is properly charged. Learn them. Use them.
POE oil absorbs water.
If you leave a system open to air, the oil will absorb moisture and destroy the compressor. Pull a deep vacuum. Always.
Recovery cylinders are gray with a yellow top.
Never mix refrigerants in a recovery cylinder. Label everything.
The customer is part of the system.
If they close vents, block returns, or set the thermostat wrong, the system will fail. Ask questions. Listen.
A2L refrigerants are mildly flammable.
Use A2L-rated tools, leak detectors, and follow the charge limits. Store cylinders upright.
Ammonia (R‑717) is toxic.
If you smell it, evacuate and call a specialist. You do not work on ammonia systems unless you're specially trained.

🛠️ Field Notes — What To Do When You See It

Practical scenarios for the job site
R‑22 system, low charge
Do NOT top off if it's a leak. Repair the leak, recover, and recharge with a SNAP‑approved substitute (R‑407C, R‑438A, etc.).
New residential install
You must use R‑32 or R‑454B (A2L). R‑410A is no longer legal for new systems.
A2L refrigerant
Use A2L‑rated tools, leak detectors, and follow the manufacturer's charge limit. Store cylinders upright.
Recovery cylinder
Gray body, yellow top. Never mix refrigerants — label it clearly.
Glide (R‑407C, R‑448A)
Charge as a liquid. If you charge as a vapor, you'll change the blend composition and it won't work properly.
Ammonia (R‑717)
If you smell it, evacuate and call a specialist. You do NOT work on ammonia systems unless you're specially trained.
B3 refrigerant
Nope. You don't touch it. Call industrial specialists.
POE oil system
POE oil absorbs moisture. Keep the system sealed and pull a deep vacuum.
Hot room call
Check windows, doors, insulation, and duct leaks before you check the unit. 90% of problems are building problems.
Customer says "it's not keeping up"
Ask when it started, what changed, and if they've closed any vents. The answer is usually in the history.

🏗️ All Paths — Residential · Commercial · Industrial

What changes at each scale
Residential
Systems: Split systems, package units, heat pumps, mini‑splits.
Refrigerants: R‑410A → R‑32 / R‑454B (A2L).
Scale: 1.5–5 tons. Attics, crawlspaces, basements. Direct customer interaction.
Typical issues: Dirty filters, duct leaks, low charge, poor airflow.
Commercial
Systems: Rooftop units (RTUs), VRF, chillers, variable‑air‑volume (VAV).
Refrigerants: R‑410A → R‑32 / R‑454B, R‑134a → R‑1234yf, R‑123 → R‑1233zd.
Scale: 5–100+ tons. Roofs, mechanical rooms, equipment pads. Building engineers and facility managers.
Typical issues: Economizer failures, control wiring, refrigerant leaks, compressor failures.
Industrial
Systems: Centrifugal chillers, screw compressors, ammonia systems, CO₂ systems.
Refrigerants: R‑717 (ammonia), R‑744 (CO₂), R‑1233zd, R‑1234ze.
Scale: 100–1,000+ tons. Machinery rooms, outdoor skids, chemical plants. Highly regulated, hazardous materials, specialized PPE.
Typical issues: Oil management, high‑pressure cutouts, vibration, process control.
The common thread: The cycle is the same at every scale. The compressor squeezes gas. The coil dumps heat. The expansion valve drops pressure. The difference is size, pressure, and refrigerant. Learn the cycle, and you can learn any path.

📖 Acronyms Glossary

What they mean — no prior knowledge required
ODP
Ozone Depletion Potential. Measures how much a refrigerant damages the ozone layer. R‑11 = 1.0. HFCs/HFOs = 0.
GWP
Global Warming Potential. Measures how much heat a refrigerant traps compared to CO₂. CO₂ = 1. R‑410A = 2,088.
SNAP
Significant New Alternatives Policy. EPA program that approves or bans refrigerants for specific uses. If it's not SNAP‑approved, it's illegal.
ASHRAE
American Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air‑Conditioning Engineers. Sets the safety classification system (A1, A2L, etc.).
CFC
Chlorofluorocarbon. Contains chlorine. Damages ozone. Banned.
HCFC
Hydrochlorofluorocarbon. Contains chlorine. Lower ozone damage. Being phased out.
HFC
Hydrofluorocarbon. No chlorine. Zero ozone damage. High GWP. Being phased down.
HFO
Hydrofluoroolefin. No chlorine. Very low GWP. Current standard. Often A2L (mildly flammable).
POE
Polyolester oil. Used with HFCs and HFOs. Absorbs moisture — must be kept sealed.
PAG
Polyalkylene glycol oil. Used in automotive AC with R‑134a and R‑1234yf.
