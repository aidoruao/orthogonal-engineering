📖 Acronyms & History — The "Why"

Understand the terms before you memorize the numbers
ODP · Ozone Depletion Potential
Measures how much a chemical damages the Earth’s ozone layer. The scale is based on R‑11 (CFC‑11) which has an ODP of 1.0.
• ODP = 0 → safe for the ozone layer (HFCs, HFOs, Natural).
• ODP > 0 → destroys ozone (CFCs, HCFCs).
GWP · Global Warming Potential
Measures how much heat a refrigerant traps in the atmosphere compared to CO₂ over 100 years.
• CO₂ has a GWP of 1 (the baseline).
• R‑410A has a GWP of 2,088 → traps 2,088× more heat than CO₂.
• HFOs have GWP ~1 → break down quickly and trap almost no heat.
SNAP · Significant New Alternatives Policy
An EPA program created under Section 612 of the Clean Air Act (1990).
It reviews and approves substitutes for ozone‑depleting substances. If a refrigerant isn’t SNAP‑approved for a specific use, it’s illegal to use it in that application.
• The EPA has the legal authority to enforce SNAP rules.
• SNAP is why we can’t use R‑22 in new systems, and why R‑454B is the new standard.
ASHRAE Safety Classification
A two‑part code: Letter (Toxicity) + Number (Flammability).
• A = lower toxicity · B = higher toxicity.
• 1 = non‑flammable · 2L = mildly flammable · 2 = flammable · 3 = highly flammable.
Family abbreviations
• CFC = ChloroFluorocarbon — contains Chlorine → high ODP → banned.
• HCFC = HydroChloroFluorocarbon — contains Chlorine → lower ODP → phase‑out.
• HFC = HydroFluorocarbon — no Chlorine → zero ODP → high GWP → phase‑down.
• HFO = HydroFluoro‑Olefin — no Chlorine → very low GWP → current standard.
• Natural = Ammonia, CO₂, Propane — ultra‑low GWP, but often toxic or flammable.

📅 Phase‑out Timelines (USA)

When you can no longer install new systems with these refrigerants
R-22
Production banned: Jan 1, 2020. Service allowed using recycled/reclaimed.
R-410A
New systems banned: Jan 1, 2025 (some equipment) / Jan 1, 2026 (all new systems).
R-134a
New systems banned: Jan 1, 2025 for some applications.
R-404A / R-507
Production & import phased down aggressively; replaced by R‑448A / R‑449A.

🛠️ Field Notes — What To Do When You See It

Practical scenarios for the job site
R‑22 system, low charge
Do NOT top off with R‑22 if it's a leak. Repair the leak, recover, and recharge with a SNAP‑approved substitute (R‑407C, R‑438A, etc.).
New residential install
You must use R‑32 or R‑454B (A2L). R‑410A is no longer legal for new systems.
A2L refrigerant
Use A2L‑rated tools, leak detectors, and follow the manufacturer's charge limit. Store cylinders upright.
Recovery cylinder
Gray body, yellow top. Never mix refrigerants in a recovery cylinder — label it clearly.
Glide (R‑407C, R‑448A)
Charge as a liquid. If you charge as a vapor, you'll change the blend composition and it won't work properly.
Ammonia (R‑717)
If you smell it, evacuate and call a specialist. You do NOT work on ammonia systems unless you're specially trained.
B3 refrigerant
Nope. You don't touch it. Call industrial specialists.
PoE oil system
PoE oil absorbs moisture like a sponge. Keep the system sealed during service and pull a deep vacuum.
Always read the cylinder label. Colors are a visual guide, not a legal standard. SNAP status and phase‑out dates are subject to regulatory change.
